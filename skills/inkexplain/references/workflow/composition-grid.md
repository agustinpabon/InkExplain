# Composition grid

Character-independent rules for turning one idea into one readable picture: framing, focal points, balance, and the visual metaphor. Each archetype's `motion-and-acting.md` adds how these structures look with its actor, plus its own objects and actions.

## Framing

- The default canvas is 16:9 landscape. Follow the user's ratio when they ask for another (1:1 for social posts, 4:5 or 9:16 for mobile).
- Think of the canvas as a three-by-three grid. The decisive action sits in the center cell or on one of the four inner intersections, never against an edge.
- The main action fills about 40–60% of the canvas; keep at least 35% empty. Percentages here and in a pack are rough guides to how far the subject and its action extend, not measurements; when a pack gives its own figure for its character, aim for that figure and keep the whitespace rule.
- Leave one quiet cell, usually a corner, so the eye can rest.

## Focal point and flow

- One focal point per image: the place where the actor touches the object that carries the idea.
- Flow reads in one direction, usually left to right, along one main arrow, path, or row of objects that runs through the focal point.
- Inputs enter from the left third, results leave through the right third; problems and warnings sit where the flow breaks.
- Labels sit next to what they name, outside the actor's face and hands, and never across the top as a title.

## Balance

- Balance the large actor or machine with open space, not with more objects.
- Three to six drawn elements and two to six labels; every element earns its place. A heap, row, or stack of identical things counts as one element.
- Group supporting objects in one cluster rather than scattering them, unless the archetype's template says how widely to spread them.
- In comparisons, give each side the same width and line weight so the difference reads as content, not emphasis.

## Structure types

Pick exactly one per image.

| Type | Use it for | Typical layout |
|---|---|---|
| Workflow | input to output, pipelines, automation | inputs on the left, the actor transforming them in the middle, output on the right, one main flow arrow |
| System close-up | one part of a larger system: a filter, gate, or store | three to five parts; the actor works inside the decisive one |
| Before and after | chaos to order, manual to automated, stuck to moving | unstable on the left, stable on the right, the cause in the middle |
| State change | conditions such as overloaded, focused, blocked, flowing, or two to four alternatives compared side by side | two to four small states, one short label each |
| Concept metaphor | an abstract idea as one strange, memorable object | one large object or machine, a few inputs, one result |
| Layers | stacks, foundations, capability levels | an informal stack; the actor builds, props up, or inspects one layer |
| Route map | journeys, roadmaps, learning paths | one curved path with three to five stops; the actor walks it or pulls the line |
| Mini comic | attempt, failure, fix | two to four panels, one action each, no speech-bubble essays |

Name the structure type in the plan, never on the image.

## Inventing the metaphor

These tools are for an idea that has no visible form: a principle, a trade-off, a pitfall. They are not the way to picture a repository, or what a particular application does: draw the application's own things first, and reach for a metaphor only when the plain drawing cannot show the idea (`references/workflow/repository-explanation.md`, "Plan the pictures from the explanation"). Invent a fresh metaphor from the current source every time:

1. **Turn the abstract into a physical action**: stuck, leaking, sorting, weighing, filtering, fermenting, folding, bridging, unpacking, flowing back.
2. **Turn the system into a low-tech object**: a pipe, drawer, funnel, scale, ladder, mailbox, well, gate, sieve, clothesline, or an odd hand-built machine.
3. **Give the action to the actor**: the actor pulls the lever, carries the load, patches the leak, guards the gate.

Prefer objects everyone has touched over technical icons. Explain an abstract software idea with plumbing, mail, kitchens, and workshops rather than server racks, dashboards, and screenshots.

## Originality

- Calibration and gallery images show density, stroke, and character attitude. Do not reproduce their compositions unless the user asks for that specific image.
- When a theme resembles an earlier image, keep the idea and change both the main object and the action.
- Never trace a pose from a source or reference image; draw a new pose for each scene.

## Common failures

- A diagram with a character pasted beside it.
- Many nodes, arrows, and boxes: a slide, not a sketch.
- A title across the top, or the structure type written on the image.
- Two competing ideas in one frame.
- An actor standing still while the objects do all the work.
- The focal point pushed to an edge, or the canvas filled from corner to corner.
